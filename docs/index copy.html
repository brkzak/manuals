Vesuvius Challenge · villa

Finding the windings

A CT scan is a block of voxels. It does not say which voxels belong to which turn of the roll. This repo holds several ways to work that out. Each tab covers one of them. This page gives the big picture.

The problemThe scan does not know about windings

Every voxel in a scan has a brightness and nothing else. No label says "this belongs to the third winding". No record says which pieces of papyrus touch. Each method on these pages creates that missing information.

Three things make it hard:

  • Windings can be packed so tightly that the scan blurs them together.
  • The papyrus is cracked, folded and missing in places.
  • A small mistake can send a trace onto the neighboring winding with no warning. Everything after that point is wrong.

The output of every approach is the same kind of thing: surfaces, stored as tifxyz meshes, that VC3D can open and flatten.

Two directionsGrow pieces, or bend a whole

Bottom-up. Find small pieces of surface first, then grow and join them. The tracer, Lasagna, Copy Out/In, fibers and the old ThaumatoAnakalyptor pipeline work like this. They are accurate where the scan is clear. They stall, or switch windings, where it is not.

Top-down. Assume the whole scroll is one spiral, then bend that spiral until it agrees with all the evidence. The spiral fit works like this. A gap in the evidence does not stop it, because the spiral itself fills the gap.

The neural winding model sits between the two. It reads winding numbers straight out of the scan along rays, and passes them to the spiral fit.

How they connectWhat feeds what

The methods are not rivals. Several of them produce the inputs that the spiral fit consumes. Click a box to open its tab.

Test benchComparing methods fairly

bladerdeeg is a test bench for surface-fitting methods. It loads a scan with reference normals taken from surfaces that people have confirmed, runs a method, and scores the result: the distance to the reference and the angle between normals. Any new method can be added as a plugin and gets the same scoring, the same UI and the same VC3D project. Today the one method it ships with is the Lasagna fit.

Published segments are an imperfect reference. Scores are only comparable between runs on the same dataset and the same reference.

Vesuvius Challenge · villa / lasagna

The Lasagna approach

Lasagna finds the windings of a rolled-up papyrus in a CT scan. It does this in four steps. Each step below has a picture you can play with.

  1. 1Read the scan A neural network turns the CT into three hint fields.
  2. 2Start a mesh A rough stack of layers is placed around a seed point.
  3. 3Fit the mesh An optimizer moves the layers until they agree with the hints.
  4. 4Export Each layer is saved as a surface that VC3D can open.

The ideaA scroll is a stack of layers

A papyrus scroll is a long strip of papyrus rolled up around a center. Each turn of the roll is called a winding. Cut across the scroll and the windings form a spiral.

Now look at a small piece of that spiral. Over a short distance the turns run almost parallel to each other, like the layers of a lasagna. Lasagna models each small piece of scroll this way: a stack of layers, one layer for each winding. The task is to find where every layer sits in 3D.

The work is split into two programs. A neural network reads the scan (step 1). An optimizer builds the layers (steps 2 to 4). They share nothing except files on disk, so either one can be replaced without touching the other.

Step 1Read the scan

A 3D U-Net looks at the CT in small overlapping tiles. For every voxel it writes three hint values.

  • cos Is there a winding here? The value is 1 on a winding and 0 halfway between two windings. Walk across the layers and it rises and falls once per winding.
  • density How close together are the windings? It is high where they are packed tightly and low where they are far apart.
  • direction Which way does the surface face? It is stored as a line, not an arrow. For a surface, "up" and "down" mean the same thing.

The network sees the scan and nothing else. It does not know which winding is which, because cos repeats. It only knows where windings are, how far apart they are and how they are tilted. Counting windings is left to the fitter in step 3.

In the blurry spot of the example, the scan view is unclear but the cos view stays clean. The network fills that area in from its surroundings, so values there are less certain.

How the network learned this

The network was trained on scans where people had already traced surfaces by hand. From the traced surfaces, the training code works out the correct cos, density and direction for every voxel. The network is then trained to reproduce those values from the scan alone.

In practice

The whole volume is processed in overlapping tiles, on one or more GPUs. The result is written to disk as OME-Zarr volumes, plus a .lasagna.json file that lists them. A run can be resumed if it is interrupted.

python lasagna/preprocess_cos_omezarr.py predict3d \
  --input vol.zarr --output pred.lasagna.json \
  --unet-checkpoint model_best.pt --cos-scaledown 2 --scaledown 4

Step 2Start a mesh

The fitter describes the scroll with a mesh: a grid of 3D points. Lasagna keeps one grid for every winding and stacks them. Rows of a grid follow the height of the scroll. Columns follow the winding around the scroll.

To begin, the user gives a seed, a point that lies on one winding. The fitter also needs a rough first guess for the shape. This can be a cylinder or shell around the scroll center, or an existing surface. The first guess is smooth and its layers are evenly spaced, so it does not follow the real windings yet. Fixing that is step 3.

Step 3Fit the mesh

Now the optimizer moves the mesh points, a little at a time. After every step it checks how badly the mesh breaks a list of rules. The amounts are added into one number called the loss. The points then move in the direction that makes the loss smaller. This is gradient descent. There is no neural network in this step.

Press play. The three rules below are a simplified version of what the real fit does.

The rules

The real fit uses more rules than the toy. Each has a weight in the config file, so a stage can emphasize some and ignore others.

Face the right way

The layer must be square to the direction hint, so its normal lines up with the arrow.

normal

Keep the grid even

Neighboring points in a row stay about one mesh step apart, so the grid does not bunch up.

step

Stay smooth

The surface may curve but it may not crumple. Sharp bends and folds are penalized.

smooth bend

Sit on a surface

An optional second model predicts the distance to the nearest surface. Points are pulled toward distance zero.

pred_dt

Next layer, next winding

Add up the density between two layers. The total must be 1, so the next layer is exactly one winding away. This is how the fit counts windings.

winding_density

Coarse to fine

Moving single points would be slow, and a small error in one place could never pull its neighbors along. So the mesh is stored as a pyramid of five levels. The top level has only a few points and moves large regions together. Each level below adds finer detail on top of the one above.

Stages

A config file lists the stages of a fit. Each stage sets the number of steps, the learning rate (how far points may move per step), and the weight of each rule. The default init.json runs two stages:

Stage 1 1,000 steps · learning rate 0.1large moves
Stage 2 500 steps · 0.01careful moves

Step 4Export the surfaces

When the fit is done, every layer of the mesh is a surface of its own. fit2tifxyz.py writes one folder per winding in the tifxyz format. The folder holds three images, x.tif, y.tif and z.tif, plus a small meta.json.

Pixel (row, column) in those images stores the x, y and z coordinate of one mesh point. VC3D, the Volume Cartographer app, opens these surfaces directly. It can also start a fit by calling fit_service.py over HTTP.

The export converts mesh coordinates back to the voxel units of the original scan, so the surfaces line up with the full-resolution volume.

python lasagna/fit2tifxyz.py --input model.pt --output out_tifxyz/

The patch tracer

The tracer starts at one seed point and grows a surface patch outward along a predicted surface. It is part of VC3D. It is semi-automatic: it does the long stretches and a person fixes the rest.

GrowPatchCeres solvervc_grow_seg_from_seed

The ideaGrow a mesh one corner at a time

The input is a surface prediction: a volume that is 1 wherever a network thinks there is papyrus surface. On its own it is not a surface. It has holes, and in places two windings are joined. The tracer turns it into a mesh, a grid of quads, starting from a seed.

It keeps a state grid that records which corners of the mesh exist so far. Growth then repeats two phases:

  1. Add new corners greedily along the edge of the patch, for several rounds.
  2. Run the optimizer over the new region and the strip of mesh behind it, so the new corners settle onto the surface.

A set of rules of thumb decides when to accept a result and when to skip it. The optimizer is Ceres, a classical least-squares solver. The entry point is vc_grow_seg_from_seed, which calls space_tracing_quad_phys in surface_helpers.cpp.

Press play. The orange line is one row of the mesh seen from the side. It grows both ways from the seed.

In three dimensions the same thing happens on a grid. Cells are committed in rings around the seed. The optimizer works mostly on the active fringe, and sometimes on the whole patch.

The rulesWhat the optimizer balances

Each new corner is placed to balance several goals at once. The code generates these as separate loss terms, and some are only created where a location is not yet used by another term.

Follow the prediction

Corners are pulled onto the bright band of the surface prediction.

Stay smooth

Three neighboring corners should lie on a straight line unless the data says otherwise.

Keep the spacing

Corners stay about one step apart, so the mesh does not stretch or pile up.

Match the normals

A normal grid, a coarse field of local surface directions made by vc_gen_normalgrids, tells the mesh which way to face.

Resist the neighbor

A nearby winding is easy to jump onto. The tracer is set up to resist that pull.

Failure modesWhere it goes wrong, and the fix

The tracer follows a trail that the prediction only half defines. The common problems are:

  • Mergers. Two nearby windings are joined by mistake in the prediction.
  • Holes. The predicted surface disappears.
  • Sheet switches. The trace jumps from its winding to a neighboring one. Mergers often cause these, as in the figure above.

The repo's workflow for correcting a patch, as documented, goes like this:

  1. Grow a patch with vc_grow_seg_from_seed. A snapshot interval saves intermediate states.
  2. In VC3D, add one point collection for each broken area. The first point is on the correct surface near the problem. The others mark where the surface should go.
  3. Optionally mask the area that should be removed.
  4. Run the tracer again with --resume and --correct. The solver pulls the surface through the points and then keeps growing from there.

The z_min and z_max parameters limit the z range. A thin band can be used to follow one long spiral.

Copy Out/InUse one winding to find the next

Once one winding is traced well, the windings just outside and inside it usually have similar curvature. Copy Out/In uses a finished mesh as a geometric reference and moves the tracing over to the neighboring winding. In the repo this lives in vc_grow_seg_from_seed.cpp and GrowPatch.cpp.

Press the button to copy outward, one winding at a time.

The learned version

A neural version of the same idea is in vesuvius/…/neural_tracing/inference/infer_rowcol_triplet_wraps.py. A 3D network looks at a crop of the scan around the existing surface and predicts displacement vectors. These are added to the surface points to give the neighboring windings. The inference code cuts the surface into overlapping boxes, averages test-time variants of each box, and merges the results. A checkpoint called copy_displacement_latest is published, and VC3D has a Neural Tracer panel for it.

Other learned tracers in the repo

autoreg_mesh continues a partial winding one vertex at a time. For each vertex it predicts a cell in a coarse grid of volume features, a small offset inside that cell, and a stop flag.

heatmap_single_point traces a patch from a start point by taking steps, and takes a new crop of the scan every few steps.

fiber_trace_3d traces fibers. It is covered in the fibers tab.

Fibers and the atlas

A papyrus fiber stays inside one winding. A line that follows a fiber is therefore a line that belongs to a single winding. Two fibers that cross can tell you how their windings relate. The atlas collects those facts.

line annotationfiber_trace_3datlas.py

Why fibersA fiber never leaves its layer

A manufactured sheet has two layers of strips. Each layer has its own fiber direction, and the two directions are roughly perpendicular. By convention the recto fibers run lengthwise on the unrolled roll and the verso fibers run across its height. A fiber lies inside one layer and does not pass through the sheet.

That makes a traced fiber useful evidence: all of its points are on one winding. It also gives a natural grid, because horizontal and vertical fibers can serve as the rows and columns of a surface.

Fibers also cause trouble. Two fibers from different windings can lie close together and run in the same direction. Distance and direction alone cannot tell whether two fibers share a surface. A person, or a careful tracer, has to look at the surroundings.

TracingA few clicks, then the tracer fills in

In VC3D, a person places control points along a fiber with the line annotation widget. Between two control points the line can be made in different ways. It can be a smooth curve through the points (cspline), or it can be found with the predicted fields, the normals and winding density from Lasagna (lasagna and trace). The record is a vc3d_fiber JSON file with the line points, the control points and the way each segment was made.

Fibers can also be predicted. The fiber_hz_vt checkpoint is an nnU-Net trained on horizontal and vertical fibers that people traced by hand years ago on older scans. The fiber_trace package trains a model on fiber records using direction-conditioned 3D crops. Its sibling fiber_trace_3d appears as the optimizer name (native_fiber_trace3d) in saved fiber files.

The atlasFibers placed on a common map

The atlas answers one question: how many windings apart are these fibers? It keeps a base mesh, taken from one of the shell_*.tifxyz surfaces of a Lasagna fit. Each fiber is projected onto that mesh using the Lasagna normals. The projection gives every point on the fiber two coordinates on the base mesh, atlasU and atlasV. Those never change.

What changes is an integer for each fiber, its winding_offset. A fiber's real position is its stored position plus the offset times the period, the width of one winding on the base mesh:

actual_atlasU = anchor.atlasU + winding_offset * period_columns

Offsets come from links. A person inspects a place where two fibers cross and picks one of three answers: same winding, different winding, or hard to say. Accepting "same winding" adds a link with a winding difference of 0. The layout step starts at a root fiber with offset 0 and walks through the links, giving every fiber the offset its links imply.

The layout treats the links as an undirected graph. Fibers that no link reaches keep offset 0. A cycle of links that disagree is reported as a conflict, and the assignment stays deterministic. The details are in atlas_implementation.md.

OutputEvidence for the global fit

Fibers do not make surfaces on their own. They make winding constraints: lines that share a winding, and pairs of fibers that are a known number of windings apart. The spiral fit takes them as input. Lasagna can also grow a patch around a fiber, and the patch can then go into the fit.

The tracer is not fully automatic. A person drives it through the line annotation widget in VC3D, and the atlas workflow needs a person's decision at every crossing.

spiral-fitting · PyTorch · NVIDIA GPU

The spiral fit

Most tools build surfaces piece by piece and end up with a pile of pieces. The spiral fit works the other way. It takes the whole pile, patches, fibers and annotations alike, and bends one ideal spiral until it agrees with as much of it as possible.

fit_spiral.pyPython 3.14whole scroll

The ideaAn ideal scroll, then a smooth crush

A scroll started as one long, nearly rectangular sheet, rolled up. Seen from the end that is a spiral. The fit models it as an Archimedean spiral, where the radius grows in proportion to the angle:

r(θ) = ω · θ / 2π

One number, ω, is the distance from one winding to the next. A point on the ideal sheet is addressed by the angle θ along the spiral and the height z. Together they give a 3D point.

The eruption crushed that spiral. The fit models the damage as a diffeomorphism: a smooth map of space that can be undone. It cannot tear the sheet, push it through itself or squash it to a point. If it starts as a spiral, it is still a spiral afterward. Because it can be undone, any point in the scan maps back onto the ideal sheet. That reverse map is the virtual unrolling.

The deformation has three parts, applied in order: a coarse scale and shear, the flow of a smooth velocity field (the main part), and a local change of the gap between windings. The flow works like dust in a steady wind. Every point rides along the arrows for one unit of time. Running the wind backward gives the exact inverse. The same machinery is used in medical image registration. In the code the flow is integrated with a few Runge-Kutta steps.

InputsAny evidence, in any mix

Each kind of evidence becomes a loss term that says what the deformed spiral should look like. No single kind has to cover the scroll.

Surface patches

tifxyz meshes from the tracer, Lasagna or Copy Out/In. A patch should coincide with a single winding over its whole extent. Verified patches are best.

Same-winding lines

Point collections or traced fibers. All their points land on one winding, whichever it is.

Relative winding

Points marked as a known number of windings apart, such as "exactly one wrap from that point".

Absolute winding

Points marked with a winding number, such as "this patch is on winding 20". They fix the global numbering.

Predicted fields

Lasagna normals and gradient magnitude (how dense the windings are), plus skeleton tracks taken from a surface prediction.

Scroll structure

The umbilicus, the central axis as a function of z. It is required. An outer shell mesh is optional and pins down where the spiral ends.

The winding annotations are what make the biggest difference. The project's own view is that short, accurate winding constraints, made at scale, are the fastest route to unrolling many scrolls, because the global fit supplies the rest. See the winding constraints page.

FittingGradient descent on the deformation

Fitting is an inverse problem. The unknowns are ω and the parameters of the deformation. All of them are optimized together with Adam, minimizing a weighted sum of the loss terms. Some examples:

  • Points from a same-winding line must land on some winding surface, and the same one.
  • Two points marked k windings apart must land exactly k windings apart.
  • A verified patch must coincide with one winding.
  • The innermost winding wraps the umbilicus and the outermost follows the outer shell.
  • Regularization terms keep the sheet from distorting.

The toy below fits a small version of that. Colored pieces are evidence: each one is a stretch of a known winding. The grey line is where the windings really are. The fit never sees it. Slide the coverage down to give the fit less evidence, and watch the spiral bridge the gaps.

OutputOne mesh per winding

The fit samples each winding of the fitted spiral on a regular grid of angle and height, pushes the samples through the deformation into scan coordinates, and writes each winding as a tifxyz mesh. Two versions are written for each winding:

  • wNNN: the pure fitted surface.
  • wNNN_spliced: the same, with the geometry of verified patches spliced in wherever the fit and the patch agree. More accurate locally where trusted geometry exists.

Because these are ordinary tifxyz files, everything downstream works as usual: load them in VC3D, flatten them, and render surface volumes for ink detection. The fit also writes a checkpoint, and statistics on how much of each kind of evidence the final surface honors. For PHerc. Paris 4 (Scroll 1), the published fit has 130 windings.

Run itFrom a dataset to meshes

It needs Python 3.14 or newer and an NVIDIA GPU. The inputs come as a dataset folder with a conventional layout, plus a small spiral-scroll.json that records the voxel size and which way the spiral turns outward (CW or ACW). A person reads that direction off the scan in VC3D.

cd spiral-fitting
uv sync
python fit_spiral.py --dataset ./spiral_datasets/phercparis4

The run does 30,000 optimization steps by default. Settings are a flat set of named options in config.py, overridden as JSON in the FIT_SPIRAL_CONFIG_OVERRIDES variable. A first run on a small slice range, for example 1,000 slices, is a good start on a smaller GPU. The spiral_service.py script runs a fit session as a service for the Spiral workspace in VC3D.

Where the evidence is thin or the scan is ambiguous, the fit is under-constrained. It may then not follow the true sheets. Verified, hard constraints are preferred over soft ones.

The full tutorial is 38_tutorial_spiral.md. The older paper describes a fully automatic version that fits only raw surface-prediction tracks.

vesuvius · neural_tracing · winding_models

The winding model

Most methods find surfaces and then count them. The winding model reads the count directly. A neural network looks at a thin 3D slab of the scan along a ray and predicts how many windings the ray passes through. Many rays together give a dense map of winding numbers.

infer_winding_volume.pytrain_winding_model.pyfeeds the spiral fit

One rayA phase that counts windings

The input is one slab of the scan, sampled around a ray. The ray is cast along the surface normal, so it crosses the windings one after another. The slab is narrow across the ray and long along it.

For every column of voxels in the slab, the network predicts a phase: a number that goes up along the ray. It rises by 1 across each winding, so every time it passes a whole number the ray is crossing a winding. The network makes the phase increase by predicting only positive steps and adding them up. Each step equals the winding density over a short stretch of the ray.

The phase has one unknown shift, because the network cannot know the winding's absolute number. The shift is fixed by a seed: a point on a fitted spiral, where the winding number is known. The ray is built so that the seed's winding is at its midpoint. Once the seed crossing is named, every other crossing is numbered from it.

Many raysRays that agree with each other

The inference script casts rays from a fitted spiral. It places seeds on the fitted surface every few windings, at a regular spacing along the winding and along z. Each ray gives crossings with absolute winding numbers. The crossings are stored as points with a number and a confidence, and a vote over all overlapping slabs fills a volume with winding numbers.

There is a catch. The seed's number comes from the spiral, and where the spiral is locally wrong, the number is wrong, and so is every crossing on that ray. A wrong ray is off by a whole winding. It looks fine on its own.

The fix uses the overlaps. Neighboring slabs see the same sheets. If two rays pass through the same sheet but give it different numbers, one of them is off. An optional mode, --phase-registration overlap, finds nearby seeds in world coordinates, compares their phase fields where they overlap, and solves one correction per slab. The graph has no edges that come from winding adjacency or from the spiral. The seed's winding is only a weak hint to settle the overall numbering.

OutputPoints, a volume, and supervision

One run of infer_winding_volume.py writes a single Zarr group:

  • winding and confidence: a volume of winding numbers, and how much to trust each. Only the chunks that were inferred exist.
  • points/* and strips/*: one record for every crossing, and the column each came from.

From there the output goes three ways.

  • Into the spiral fit. export_spiral_supervision.py turns the crossings into a compact store. The fit then trains on long relative-winding pairs and on pairs of adjacent crossings. A pair of adjacent crossings gives the density: one winding divided by the gap.
  • Into a global field. merge_winding_field.py reconciles billions of crossing points into one smooth winding field by gradient descent, and writes a sparse OME-Zarr pyramid that VC3D can stream. A winding field on a connected spiral must have one thin seam per turn, where the number jumps. The script reports the seam and does not hide it.
  • Into centerlines. winding_centerlines.py reduces each winding label to one ordered line per z plane, and can export them as tifxyz.

Running the network is the slow part. For this reason there is a native phase cache: inference can stop after the network and save its raw phase output. The reconstruction can then be run again, with different settings, without the network. See NATIVE_PHASE_CACHE.md.

What the network looks like

The input slab is sampled around the ray, with two short transverse axes and a long axis along the ray. A 3D convolutional encoder halves only the transverse axes, so features keep their position along the ray. Blocks of axial attention then do the long-range work: one attention along the ray for each column, and one across the transverse plane for each position along the ray. A light decoder restores the transverse resolution.

Every prediction head runs per column, so the network predicts winding structure across the whole slab, not just on the central ray. The central ray is only the sampling frame. The native output is 32 × 32 × 384 values per slab. Older checkpoints also have a crossing head and a variance head. Newer ones decode crossings from the phase alone.

The winding model needs a fitted spiral to cast its rays from, and a rays-only view cannot recover a region that no ray passed through. Check the registration statistics before treating a result as final.

deprecated/thaumato-anakalyptor · 2023 Grand Prize

ThaumatoAnakalyptor

An older, self-contained pipeline that goes from a raw scan to a mesh with almost no human input. It finds surface points, groups them into patches, then works out which winding each patch is on. It is kept in the repo under deprecated/.

deprecatedpoint cloudgraph solver

The pipelinePoints, patches, graph, mesh

  1. 1Surface points. Use 3D derivatives of brightness to find the papyrus surface voxels.
  2. 2Patches. Split the point cloud into blocks and cluster the points into patches with a 3D instance segmentation network (Mask3D).
  3. 3Graph. Make a node for each patch and an edge wherever two patches overlap.
  4. 4Windings. Solve for the winding angle of every node, so each patch is placed on its turn of the roll.
  5. 5Mesh. Rebuild a surface from the points of one sheet with Poisson reconstruction, then texture it with Volume Cartographer's renderer.

Step 1Finding the surface with derivatives

Seen from the side, a papyrus sheet is a bell-shaped bump in voxel brightness. The 3D gradient of the volume gives the local normal of the sheet. Along that normal, the first and second derivatives of brightness tell where the bump is. Thresholding on them marks surface voxels, and the marked voxels form a point cloud with a normal at every point.

Steps 3 and 4The sheet stitching problem

After clustering, there are thousands of patches. Neighbors along a sheet overlap, and so do neighbors on adjacent windings. Each edge in the graph carries a hint about how the two winding angles relate. Along a sheet the angle changes a little. Across to the next winding it changes by 360°.

The unknown is the absolute winding angle of every node. A first guess comes from spreading values out along a spanning tree. That guess is often wrong: one bad edge sends a whole branch onto the wrong winding. The solver then repairs it. Two solvers are in the repo, a spring-based graph solver in C++ and a GPU belief-propagation solver with iterated MAP estimation and Laplacian smoothing.

Run itHeavy, and mostly historical

The pipeline needs an RTX 4090 class GPU with 24 GB, about 196 GB of RAM plus swap, and at least 32 CPU threads. The precomputation alone is expected to take days. Intermediate data takes about twice the space of the scan. It also works on 8 µm grid cells, so 4 µm scans are halved first.

The commands run in order: generate_half_sized_grid, grid_to_pointcloud, pointcloud_to_instances, instances_to_graph, the C++ graph_problem solver, graph_to_mesh and large_mesh_to_surface. The project README has the full list, with arguments.

The repo's own TODO says that a better sheet-stitching algorithm is the thing to implement next. Later work in this repo, the spiral fit above all, takes a different route to the same problem.
